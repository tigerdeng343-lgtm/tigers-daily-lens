## 🤖 OpenAI vs. Anthropic: The Super Bowl Showdown

The AI rivalry just got *spicy*. Anthropic is running Super Bowl ads this Sunday that mock OpenAI's decision to put advertisements in ChatGPT, featuring "unnaturally effusive" chatbots that creepily befriend users before hawking products.

The punchline: **"Ads are coming to AI. But not to Claude."**

Sam Altman's response on X? He laughed—then clapped back: *"Anthropic serves an expensive product to rich people."* He boasted that more Texans use ChatGPT free than all Americans who use Claude.

**The bigger picture:** Both companies are racing toward enterprise clients and potential IPOs while burning billions on compute. Neither is profitable, but investors keep betting the frontier AI race justifies "extraordinary capital intensity."

Meanwhile, OpenAI launched "Frontier"—an enterprise platform to become the one-stop AI shop—and Anthropic unveiled Claude Opus 4.6, claiming better agentic capabilities. OpenAI countered hours later with an upgraded Codex.

**The lesson:** When you're losing billions, attack ads are cheaper than R&D.

## 🚀 Musk Builds His Empire: SpaceX Swallows xAI

Elon Musk merged SpaceX and xAI this week, combining rockets, Starlink, X (Twitter), and the Grok chatbot into a single mega-company. The play? **Space-based AI data centers.**

*"It's always sunny in space!"* Musk wrote, predicting that within 2-3 years, the lowest-cost way to run AI compute will be in orbit.

Microsoft's Brad Smith is skeptical: *"I'll be surprised if people move from land to low-Earth orbit."*

**The plot twist:** Democratic Senators Warren and Kim wrote to Defense Secretary Hegseth expressing concern that **Chinese investment in SpaceX** could pose "a national security threat, potentially jeopardizing key military, intelligence, and civilian infrastructure."

With SpaceX handling Pentagon launches and Starlink providing battlefield internet in Ukraine, the scrutiny is understandable. Musk's empire is valued at $768 billion—and increasingly intertwined with U.S. national security.

## 🚗 Stellantis Takes a €22.2 Billion EV Reality Check

Remember when every automaker was going all-in on EVs? Stellantis (Chrysler, Jeep, Peugeot, Fiat) just announced **€22.2 billion in charges** as it scales back its electric vehicle ambitions.

Translation: *The EV transition is harder and more expensive than the PowerPoints promised.*

Shares got hammered. The company joins a growing list of legacy automakers recalibrating EV timelines as:
- Consumer demand softens
- Chinese competitors (BYD, etc.) dominate on cost
- Battery supply chains remain expensive

**Meanwhile in Tokyo:** Toyota named Kenta Kon as next CEO—a finance chief and close ally of Chairman Akio Toyoda. Toyota's been the cautious voice on EVs, betting on hybrids while others charged ahead. That bet is looking smarter by the day.

## 💰 Amazon Drops a $200 Billion Bomb

Amazon surprised Wall Street by announcing **$200 billion in planned capex for 2026**—the biggest single-year spend in its history.

Where's it going? AI infrastructure: data centers, chips, and the compute backbone to power AWS and Alexa's next evolution.

This is Amazon saying: *"We're not ceding AI ground to Microsoft or Google."*

For perspective, that's more than the GDP of most countries. The "AI arms race" is no longer a metaphor—it's a line item.

## 🌍 Iran-US Nuclear Talks: High Stakes in Oman

Iran and the United States opened high-stakes negotiations in Oman today over Tehran's nuclear program. The talks come as Trump has warned military options remain "on the table."

**Why it matters for markets:** A breakdown could mean another Middle East conflict, oil supply disruptions, and risk-off sentiment. Success could ease tensions but faces long odds—disputes over broadening the agenda have already threatened to derail diplomacy.

Energy traders are watching closely.

## 🍎 FDA Relaxes "No Artificial Colors" Labels

In a win for RFK Jr.'s agenda, the FDA now allows food companies to claim "no artificial colors" even when using natural dyes derived from plants (like beetroot red or spirulina).

The FDA also approved two new natural colorants and is reviewing the six remaining petroleum-based dyes still in the U.S. food supply.

**Market angle:** Consumer brands like PepsiCo and Nestle have already been reformulating. This rule change accelerates the shift and may help marketing claims—but critics warn it could mislead consumers about additives like titanium dioxide.
